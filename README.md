# CS 230 Final Project
Formula 1 is often described as one of the most data-rich sports in the world: every car streams gigabytes of telemetry per weekend, yet most quantitative work still models performance at the level of lap times, points, or race results rather than as the outcome of a continuous control process. This leaves a central question under-explored: to what extent does a driver’s individual 'style', such as their characteristic braking, throttle, and gear usage explain performance once the team/car is accounted for? Motivated by this gap, we use 2022--2024 qualifying telemetry from FastF1 to study whether there exists a low-dimensional, team-invariant driver-style embedding that is both predictive of driver identity and largely uninformative about the constructor. We construct fixed-length windows of five normalised channels (speed, throttle, brake, gear, RPM) and train four convolutional encoder-based models: a residual metric-learning encoder, a purely supervised encoder, and two conditional VAEs with adversarial team disentanglement. We then evaluate the learned embeddings through inter-driver centroid distances and simple linear probes for team and driver recoverability. Across all models we observe a consistent pattern: when embeddings are strongly predictive of the driver, they are also strongly predictive of the team; when team information is suppressed, driver predictability collapses toward chance. These results suggest that, in modern F1 qualifying telemetry, team and car effects dominate and driver style operates only in relatively narrow, team-coupled margins, extending Bell’s multilevel evidence on race outcomes to the telemetry domain.
